1 Moving the plane

A point of the plane is a pair of numbers \(p = (x, y)\), which we also write as a column \(p = \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}\) and treat as a vector: points can be added and multiplied by numbers. Every figure in the app is made by moving a starting shape (on the 2D page, by default, the unit square with corners \((0,0)\), \((1,0)\), \((1,1)\), \((0,1)\)) in a few simple ways.

Translations

The simplest move is a translation: every point is moved by the same vector \(b = (b_1, b_2)\), \[ T_b(p) = p + b. \] Translations keep lengths, angles and orientation. Two of them in a row make another, \(T_b \circ T_c = T_{b+c}\), and the order does not matter. A translation by \(b \neq 0\) moves every point, so it leaves none where it is; we will meet maps that do leave one point fixed in section 2.

Linear maps and matrices

The other moves keep the origin where it is, and send straight lines to straight lines and the midpoint of two points to the midpoint of their images. Such a map is linear: it is \(p \mapsto A p\) for a \(2 \times 2\) matrix \[ A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a x + b y \\ c x + d y \end{pmatrix}. \] The matrix is easy to read: its first column \((a, c)\) is where the point \((1, 0)\) goes and its second column \((b, d)\) is where \((0, 1)\) goes. So the unit square is sent to the parallelogram with those two columns as sides. Its area is the absolute value of the determinant, \(\det A = ad - bc\); if the determinant is negative, the square has been flipped over.

The moves of the app are these matrices:

  • Rotation by an angle \(\theta\) (anticlockwise): \( R(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix} \). Its columns are \((\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\), the point \((1,0)\) turned by \(\theta\), and \((-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)\), the point \((0,1)\) turned by \(\theta\). Rotations keep lengths (\(R^\top R = I\)), have determinant \(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1\), and add up: \(R(\alpha) R(\beta) = R(\alpha + \beta)\).
  • Scaling by a factor \(s\): \(s I = \begin{pmatrix} s & 0 \\ 0 & s \end{pmatrix}\), which multiplies every length by \(s\) and every area by \(s^2\).
  • Stretch: \(\operatorname{diag}(1, k) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & k \end{pmatrix}\) scales heights by \(k\) and leaves widths alone.
  • Slant (a shear) by an angle \(\varphi\): \( \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \tan\varphi \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \), which leans the upright sides of the square over by \(\varphi\) and keeps its base and its area.
  • Mirror: \(\operatorname{diag}(1, -1)\) flips the plane over the horizontal axis; its determinant is \(-1\).

In each picture the dashed square is the unit square and the green figure its image; the dot marks where the corner \((0,0)\) goes and the thick side is the image of the base. This is exactly the green frame the app draws for every transform.

Affine maps, and why the order matters

A linear map followed by a translation is an affine map, \[ f(p) = A p + b . \tag{1.1} \] It is given by six numbers: the four of \(A\) and the two of \(b\). The image of the unit square is the parallelogram with corners \(b\), \(b + A e_1\), \(b + A e_1 + A e_2\) and \(b + A e_2\), where \(e_1 = (1,0)\) and \(e_2 = (0,1)\). Two affine maps in a row give an affine map again: \[ (f \circ g)(p) = A_f \,(A_g\, p + b_g) + b_f = (A_f A_g)\, p + (A_f\, b_g + b_f). \tag{1.2} \] Matrix products do not commute, so the order matters, even for a turn and a move:

This rule is how the app computes: a copy of a copy is the product of the two maps by (1.2), six multiplications and a few additions, and the starting shape is drawn through it.

The transform boxes of the 2D page

Each transform box of the 2D page holds a position \(X, Y\), a rotation \(\theta\), a scale \(s\), a stretch \(k\) (height divided by width), a slant \(\varphi\) and whether it is mirrored (\(m = -1\), else \(m = 1\)). They make the affine map with \[ A = R(\theta) \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & m \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \tan\varphi \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} s & 0 \\ 0 & k s \end{pmatrix}, \qquad b = \begin{pmatrix} X \\ Y \end{pmatrix}. \tag{1.3} \] Read from the right: the square is scaled (its height \(k\) times its width), slanted, mirrored over its base, turned, and moved so that its corner \((0,0)\) lands on \((X, Y)\). Its area is multiplied by \(|\det A| = k s^2\). When \(k = 1\) and \(\varphi = 0\), the map multiplies every distance by the same factor, \(|f(p) - f(q)| = s\,|p - q|\), and the copy has exactly the shape of the original: it is a similarity. That is the "similar" in the app's name; stretch and slant give the more general self-affine figures.

2 Shrinking: contractions and fixed points

A map \(f\) is a contraction if it brings any two points closer together by at least a fixed factor \(r < 1\): \[ |f(p) - f(q)| \le r\,|p - q| \quad \text{for all } p, q. \] For an affine map, \(f(p) - f(q) = A(p - q)\), so the best such \(r\) is the most that \(A\) stretches any vector, its operator norm \(\lVert A \rVert\). It is the largest singular value \(\sigma_1\) of \(A\): the matrix sends the unit circle to an ellipse, and \(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2\) are the lengths of its two half-axes. For a similarity of scale \(s\), both are \(s\). For the matrix \(A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}\), \[ \sigma_{1,2}^2 = \frac{P \pm \sqrt{P^2 - 4\,(\det A)^2}}{2}, \qquad P = a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + d^2 , \] the formula the app uses; and \(\sigma_1 \sigma_2 = |\det A|\).

Banach's fixed point theorem (1922). A contraction \(f\) of the plane (or of any complete metric space) has exactly one fixed point \(p^*\), with \(f(p^*) = p^*\). From any starting point, the sequence \(p, f(p), f(f(p)), \dots\) tends to it, and \[ |f^n(p) - p^*| \le r^n\, |p - p^*| . \]

The proof is short. Uniqueness: if \(p^*\) and \(q^*\) were both fixed, \(|p^* - q^*| = |f(p^*) - f(q^*)| \le r\,|p^* - q^*|\), which forces \(|p^* - q^*| = 0\). Existence: the steps \(|f^{n+1}(p) - f^n(p)| \le r^n |f(p) - p|\) shrink like a geometric series, so the sequence is a Cauchy sequence and has a limit; the limit is fixed because \(f\) is continuous. The estimate follows from \(|f^n(p) - p^*| = |f^n(p) - f^n(p^*)| \le r^n |p - p^*|\).

For an affine contraction, the fixed point solves \(A p + b = p\): \[ p^* = (I - A)^{-1}\, b . \] The Heart spiral preset is a single transform, a turn of \(20^\circ\) and a scale of 0.9. Its generations are the heart, its image, the image of that, and so on: they spiral in towards \(p^* \approx (-0.454,\ 0.715)\), each 0.9 times the size of the one before. The limit of the whole construction is that single point, which is why the app gives its dimension as 0.

3 Many copies: iterated function systems

With one contraction everything shrinks to a point. With several, something much richer appears. An iterated function system (IFS) is a finite list of contractions \(f_1, \dots, f_N\): the transforms of the app. For any figure \(S\), let \[ W(S) = f_1(S) \cup f_2(S) \cup \dots \cup f_N(S) , \tag{3.1} \] the figure made of its \(N\) copies. \(W\) is the Hutchinson operator. Starting from a shape \(S_0\), the app draws its generations \[ S_{n+1} = W(S_n), \qquad S_n = \bigcup_{i_1, \dots, i_n} f_{i_1} \circ f_{i_2} \circ \dots \circ f_{i_n} (S_0) . \] Generation \(n\) is made of \(N^n\) copies of the starting shape, one for each list of \(n\) transforms; with all generations shown there are \(1 + N + \dots + N^n = \frac{N^{n+1} - 1}{N - 1}\) of them, the number the app counts when it limits Repeat.

To say in what sense \(S_n\) tends to a limit, we need a distance between figures. The Hausdorff distance between two closed and bounded sets \(S\) and \(T\) is the least \(\varepsilon\) such that each lies within \(\varepsilon\) of the other: \[ d_H(S, T) = \max\Bigl\{ \sup_{s \in S} \operatorname{dist}(s, T),\ \sup_{t \in T} \operatorname{dist}(t, S) \Bigr\} . \] With it, the nonempty closed and bounded sets form a complete metric space, and \(W\) is a contraction there: if every \(f_i\) has factor at most \(r\), then \[ d_H\bigl(W(S), W(T)\bigr) \le r\, d_H(S, T) , \] because each copy \(f_i(S)\) lies within \(r\, d_H(S,T)\) of \(f_i(T)\). Banach's theorem, applied to \(W\), gives at once:

Hutchinson's theorem (1981). An iterated function system of contractions has exactly one nonempty closed and bounded set \(\mathcal{A}\) with \[ \mathcal{A} = f_1(\mathcal{A}) \cup \dots \cup f_N(\mathcal{A}) , \] its attractor; and from any nonempty closed and bounded starting shape, \(d_H(S_n, \mathcal{A}) \le r^n\, d_H(S_0, \mathcal{A}) \to 0\).

The equation says that the attractor is made of \(N\) smaller copies of itself: it is self-similar (or self-affine), and every piece, magnified, shows the whole again. It also explains the picture: the starting shape only decides what the early generations look like, and is forgotten at the rate \(r^n\). With the Last generation only box ticked, the app shows \(S_n\), an approximation of \(\mathcal{A}\) to within \(r^n\) of its size; with it unticked, it shows all of \(S_0, \dots, S_n\) together, which is how the trees are usually drawn.

A point of the attractor can be named by an infinite list of transforms \(i_1 i_2 i_3 \dots\), its address: \[ p = \lim_{n \to \infty} f_{i_1} \circ f_{i_2} \circ \dots \circ f_{i_n}(x) , \] the same limit for every starting point \(x\). In the Sierpinski triangle, the address is a route: first which of the three corners' triangles the point is in, then which of the three inside that, and so on, like the digits of a number in base 3.

4 Dimension

A segment, a square and a cube have dimensions 1, 2 and 3. One way to see the number: cut the figure into small pieces of size \(\varepsilon\) and count them. A segment needs about \(1/\varepsilon\) pieces, a square \(1/\varepsilon^2\), a cube \(1/\varepsilon^3\). For a figure that needs about \(N(\varepsilon) \approx C\, \varepsilon^{-D}\) pieces, \(D\) is its (box-counting) dimension: \[ D = \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0} \frac{\log N(\varepsilon)}{\log(1/\varepsilon)} . \]

Self-similar figures

For an attractor made of \(N\) copies of itself, each scaled by \(r\), the counting is easy: covering it with pieces of size \(r\varepsilon\) takes \(N\) times as many as with pieces of size \(\varepsilon\), since each copy is a small version of the whole. So \(C (r\varepsilon)^{-D} = N C \varepsilon^{-D}\), that is \(N r^D = 1\), and \[ D = \frac{\log N}{\log (1/r)} . \tag{4.1} \] A square is 4 copies of half its size: \(\log 4/\log 2 = 2\), as it should be. The Sierpinski triangle is 3 copies of half its size: \(D = \log 3/\log 2 \approx 1.585\), more than a curve, less than a surface. The Koch curve is 4 copies of a third: \(\log 4/\log 3 \approx 1.262\).

When the copies have different scales \(r_1, \dots, r_N\), the same argument gives Moran's equation (1946): \(D\) is the one number with \[ r_1^D + r_2^D + \dots + r_N^D = 1 . \tag{4.2} \] The left side decreases from \(N\) (at \(D = 0\)) towards 0, so there is exactly one solution; the app finds it by bisection. For the leaning tree, with \(r_1 = \cos 30^\circ\) and \(r_2 = \sin 30^\circ\), the solution is \(D = 2\), because \(\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1\). (The app shows 1.9998: it stores \(\cos 30^\circ\) as 0.8660. Likewise it stores \(\tfrac13\) as 0.3333, which turns the Koch curve's 1.2619 into 1.2617.)

The answer is the similarity dimension. It equals the Hausdorff dimension of the attractor, the most careful notion of fractional dimension, when the copies do not overlap too much (Moran and Hutchinson's open set condition: there is an open set \(U\) whose images \(f_i(U)\) lie inside \(U\) and do not meet). When the copies overlap, (4.2) only gives an upper bound. The app's maple leaf shows about 2.38: a figure in the plane cannot have dimension above 2, so the number simply says that its copies overlap. Its true dimension is 2.

Self-affine figures

A stretched or slanted copy shrinks by different amounts in different directions, by its singular values \(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2\). Covering a thin ellipse of half-axes \(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2\) by squares of side \(\sigma_2\) takes about \(\sigma_1/\sigma_2\) of them. Falconer (1988) turned this into the singular value function \[ \varphi^D(A) = \begin{cases} \sigma_1^{D} & 0 \le D \le 1, \\ \sigma_1\, \sigma_2^{\,D-1} & 1 \le D \le 2, \\ (\sigma_1 \sigma_2)^{D/2} = |\det A|^{D/2} & D \ge 2, \end{cases} \] and the affinity dimension, the \(D\) with \[ \sum_{i=1}^{N} \varphi^D(A_i) = 1 . \tag{4.3} \] For similarities \(\varphi^D(A) = s^D\), and (4.3) is (4.2). Falconer proved that (4.3) gives the Hausdorff dimension of the attractor for almost every choice of the translations, when every \(\sigma_1 < \tfrac13\) (Solomyak, 1998, extended this to \(\sigma_1 < \tfrac12\)); in general it is an upper bound. This is the number the app shows for stretched or slanted transforms, such as the fern's 1.866. In \(d\) dimensions, with singular values \(\sigma_1 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_d\) and \(k = \lfloor D \rfloor\), \[ \varphi^D(A) = \sigma_1 \cdots \sigma_k\, \sigma_{k+1}^{\,D-k} \quad (D < d), \qquad \varphi^D(A) = |\det A|^{D/d} \quad (D \ge d), \] which is how the 3D and 4D pages compute it.

5 The chaos game

Drawing generations takes \(N^n\) copies, which grows very fast. The Random dots drawing mode uses another idea, Barnsley's chaos game. Start from any point \(x_0\), and repeat: pick one of the transforms at random, the transform \(i\) with probability \(p_i\), and move the point, \[ x_{n+1} = f_{i_{n+1}}(x_n) . \] Put a dot wherever the point lands. After a few steps every dot lies on the attractor, up to an error that shrinks like \(r^n\): \(x_n = f_{i_n} \circ \dots \circ f_{i_1}(x_0)\) is within \(r^n \operatorname{dist}(x_0, \mathcal{A})\) of the point of \(\mathcal{A}\) with the address \(i_n i_{n-1} \dots\). The dots then wander over the whole attractor: with probability 1, every finite address, and so every part of \(\mathcal{A}\), comes up again and again.

How often the dots visit each part is decided by the probabilities. Elton's ergodic theorem (1987) says that the share of dots in a region tends to its weight under one particular measure \(\mu\) on the attractor, the one with \[ \mu = \sum_{i=1}^{N} p_i\ \mu \circ f_i^{-1} . \] A copy \(f_i(\mathcal{A})\) has a share \(p_i\) of the dots and an area in proportion to \(|\det A_i|\). To cover the figure evenly, the app follows Barnsley's recipe, \[ p_i \propto \max\bigl(|\det A_i|,\ \delta\bigr) , \] with a small floor \(\delta\) (2% of the average) so that a flat transform, such as the fern's stem with \(\det A \approx 0\), still gets a few dots. When the view is zoomed in, the app goes further: it cuts the attractor into small pieces \(f_{i_1} \circ \dots \circ f_{i_k}(\mathcal{A})\), keeps those that reach into the window, and plays the game inside them, so that no dots are wasted off screen.

Each preset of the 2D page is a small IFS. Unless said otherwise, its transforms are similarities and the dimension is given by (4.1) or (4.2). A click on a picture opens it in the app, where the green frames show the transforms.

7 Three dimensions

Nothing in sections 2 to 5 used the plane in particular: they hold word for word in space, with points \(p = (x, y, z)\), \(3 \times 3\) matrices and affine maps \(f(p) = A p + b\), now given by twelve numbers. The starting shape of the 3D page is the unit cube \([0,1]^3\).

Turning in space

In space, a turn happens about an axis. About the three coordinate axes, \[ R_x(\alpha) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \cos\alpha & -\sin\alpha \\ 0 & \sin\alpha & \cos\alpha \end{pmatrix}, \quad R_y(\beta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\beta & 0 & \sin\beta \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -\sin\beta & 0 & \cos\beta \end{pmatrix}, \quad R_z(\gamma) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\gamma & -\sin\gamma & 0 \\ \sin\gamma & \cos\gamma & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}. \] Unlike turns in the plane, these do not commute: a quarter turn about X and then about Y does not end where a quarter turn about Y and then about X does. So the order has to be fixed. A transform box of the 3D page holds the angles \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma\) ("Turn X, Y, Z"), the sizes \(s_x, s_y, s_z\), whether it is mirrored (\(m = \pm 1\)) and the move \(b\), and makes \[ A = R_y(\beta)\, R_x(\alpha)\, R_z(\gamma)\, \operatorname{diag}(m\, s_x,\ s_y,\ s_z) . \tag{7.1} \] Read from the right again: resize (and mirror), tilt about Z, then about X, then turn about the upright Y axis. These are Euler angles, and every turn of space can be written this way. By Euler's rotation theorem every turn of space is in fact a turn about a single axis; the three angles are just a convenient way to set it.

Seeing it on a flat screen

The picture is drawn in perspective. The view is turned (Yaw and Pitch, by dragging), then a point at depth \(z\) towards the viewer, who sits at distance \(D\), is drawn at \[ (x', y') = \frac{D}{D - z}\,(x, y) , \] so that nearer things look bigger. The cubes are painted from the farthest to the nearest (the painter's algorithm), each face only if it faces the viewer (if its outward normal \(n\) points towards the eye), and with a brightness that grows with \(n \cdot \ell\), the cosine of the angle between the face and the light \(\ell\) (Lambert's law).

8 Four dimensions

Once more the algebra does not change: a point of four-dimensional space is \(p = (x, y, z, w)\), a transform is \(f(p) = A p + b\) with a \(4 \times 4\) matrix (twenty numbers), and Hutchinson's theorem, Moran's equation and the chaos game hold as before. The new direction, W, is at right angles to X, Y and Z. What changes is that we can no longer simply look at the result.

The tesseract

The starting shape of the 4D page is the unit tesseract \([0,1]^4\), the four-dimensional cube. Its corners are the 16 points with coordinates 0 or 1. Its parts are counted in the same way as for the square and the cube: a part of dimension \(k\) is found by letting \(k\) of the four coordinates vary and fixing the others to 0 or 1, so there are \[ \binom{4}{k}\, 2^{\,4-k} \] of them: 16 corners, 32 edges, 24 squares and 8 cubes. (Check: \(16 - 32 + 24 - 8 = 0\), Euler's formula for a four-dimensional solid.) Just as a cube is bounded by 6 squares, the tesseract is bounded by 8 cubes, the cells \(x_k = 0\) and \(x_k = 1\).

Turning in four dimensions

In the plane a turn moves every point except the centre; in space it leaves an axis in place. In four dimensions a turn leaves a whole plane in place: a turn happens in a plane, not about an axis. Turning by \(\theta\) in the plane of X and W, for example, is \[ P_{xw}(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & 0 & 0 & -\sin\theta \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ \sin\theta & 0 & 0 & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}, \] which moves X towards W and leaves the YZ plane alone. There are \(\binom42 = 6\) coordinate planes, XY, XZ, XW, YZ, YW and ZW, so a transform box of the 4D page has six turns, and \[ A = P_{xy}(\theta_1)\, P_{xz}(\theta_2)\, P_{xw}(\theta_3)\, P_{yz}(\theta_4)\, P_{yw}(\theta_5)\, P_{zw}(\theta_6)\, \operatorname{diag}(m\, s_x,\ s_y,\ s_z,\ s_w) . \tag{8.1} \] Every turn of four-dimensional space can be written this way. That is how the app turns a drag on the figure, which can produce any turn \(Q\), back into six angles: it clears the entries of \(Q\) below the diagonal one by one with plane turns (Givens rotations, as in a QR factorisation), and the angles it used are the answer.

Something new can happen in four dimensions: a turn in two planes at once that have only the origin in common, such as XY and ZW. Such a double rotation leaves no point but the origin where it is, and has no axis or fixed plane at all. When both angles are equal, as in the Clifford spiral, every point turns by the same angle (an isoclinic rotation).

Seeing four dimensions

In Edwin Abbott's Flatland (1884), a sphere visits a world of two dimensions. The flat people can see only where it crosses their plane: a point that grows into a circle, then shrinks back to a point and vanishes. To them, the sphere is a circle that changes in time. We are in the same position with four dimensions, and the 4D page uses the two ways out of it, both as animations.

Slices. Show only the part of the figure where \(w\) is near a value \(w_0\), and move \(w_0\) through the figure. For the copies of the tesseract, the app cuts each one by the hyperplane \(w = w_0\): each of its eight cells that the hyperplane crosses gives one face of the cut, a polygon through the points where the hyperplane crosses the cell's edges (where the \(w\) of the two ends lies on either side of \(w_0\)).

Turning and perspective. Turn the whole figure by an angle \(\alpha\) in a plane that mixes W with a direction we see, say XW, about its centre \(c\), and look at it along W with perspective, as a camera looks along its depth: the point \(q = P_{xw}(\alpha)(p - c)\) is drawn in space at \[ c + \frac{d}{d - q_w}\,(q_x,\ q_y,\ q_z) , \qquad d = 3\rho , \tag{8.2} \] where \(\rho\) is the radius of the figure. Parts further along W (larger \(q_w\), nearer the eye) look larger. A tesseract seen this way is the well-known "cube inside a cube": the near cell is the large cube, the far cell the small one, and six cells join them. As \(\alpha\) grows, cells move from the inside to the outside: the tesseract seems to turn inside out.

With Solid shapes, only the outside of each copy is drawn, the 4D counterpart of skipping the faces of a cube that face away. Take the eye, the point \(e\) at distance \(d\) along W from the centre, and write it in the copy's own coordinates (those in which the copy, after the turn, is the unit tesseract), \(u = A^{-1}(e - b)\). The cell \(x_k = 0\) of the copy faces the eye when \(u_k < 0\), and the cell \(x_k = 1\) when \(u_k > 1\). Each square of the tesseract lies between two cells, and it is on the outline of the solid exactly when one of its two cells faces the eye and the other does not.

9 The four-dimensional presets

Each card shows the figure turned by \(30^\circ\) through W, and a slice. Several of them are built from simpler figures: the product of two self-similar sets with the same scale, \(\mathcal{A} \times \mathcal{B}\), is self-similar with \(N_{\mathcal{A}} N_{\mathcal{B}}\) copies, so its dimension is \[ \frac{\log (N_{\mathcal{A}} N_{\mathcal{B}})}{\log(1/r)} = \dim \mathcal{A} + \dim \mathcal{B} . \]
